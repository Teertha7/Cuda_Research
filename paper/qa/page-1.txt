Detection using OpenMP and CUDA

Abstract—Accelerating an edge-detection kernel does not nec-
essarily reduce host-visible image-processing latency. We charac-
terize sequential C++, OpenMP, CUDA, shared-memory Sobel,
and row-partitioned CPU–GPU Sobel on an Intel Core i7-
13700HX and NVIDIA RTX 4060 Laptop GPU. A custom
Canny variant provides a contrasting CPU workload with global
hysteresis. Three procedural grayscale families at four resolutions
yield 3780 retained observations across 252 conﬁgurations, with
output validation outside every timed interval. At 4096 × 4096,
pooled median Sobel latency is 37.318 ms sequentially, 5.996 ms
with eight OpenMP threads, and 9.839 ms for baseline CUDA
including staging and transfers. Shared-memory tiling increases
measured kernel time in the tested conﬁgurations. Hybrid results
depend on resolution and measurement context; a simple ﬁtted
partition model has 6.13% mean absolute percentage error on
the held-out largest resolution. These are bounded, single-session
characterization results, not a novel scheduler or a claim of
general GPU superiority. The accompanying artifact preserves
individual timings, source hashes, analysis scripts, and explicit
algorithm and timing contracts.
Index Terms—edge detection, Sobel, Canny, OpenMP, CUDA,

I. INTRODUCTION
Edge detection offers a useful contrast between regular
local computation and multi-stage processing with global
connectivity. A Sobel output depends on a small neigh-
borhood, making spatial partitioning straightforward. Canny
adds smoothing, nonmaximum suppression and hysteresis,
so distributing individual stages or image regions introduces
different correctness obligations. Canny’s original formulation
emphasizes detection and localization rather than a particular
parallel implementation [1].
For a discrete GPU, a short convolution can ﬁnish quickly
while host staging, transfers and synchronization dominate
application-visible latency. The CUDA programming model
exposes parallelism and memory hierarchy [2]; it does not
guarantee that ofﬂoading every image is proﬁtable. Multicore
CPU execution is an essential comparison, rather than relying
only on a sequential baseline. Benchmark diversity and com-
munication accounting are central concerns in heterogeneous
evaluation [3].
We ask how image size, CPU thread count, CUDA launch
conﬁguration and CPU row fraction affect latency under an
identical Sobel contract. The study contributes a reproducible
implementation and measurement artifact, an explicit compar-
ison of kernel and pipeline timing, and a held-out diagnostic
model of candidate row allocations. It does not claim a new
edge detector, a new CPU–GPU partitioning principle, or an
exhaustive optimization search. Prior work already addresses
adaptive mapping and cooperative image processing. Our
scope is a controlled course-project characterization on one
commodity laptop.
II. RELATED WORK
Luo and Duraiswami implement complete CUDA Canny,
including hysteresis, and compare against CPU software [4].
Emrani et al. evaluate several detectors and replace a breadth-
ﬁrst procedure with a parallel approach [5]. Mochurad studies
CUDA Canny with image-size and noise-related analysis [6].
Song et al. compare serial, OpenMP, CUDA and OpenCL
Canny and investigate access and launch organization [7].
These works preclude a novelty claim based simply on GPU
edge detection or comparing programming models. Canny
variants also differ in smoothing, thresholding and connec-
tivity; published speedups are not directly transferable to our
implementation.
Chang et al. optimize a four-direction 5 × 5 Sobel op-
erator using warp primitives and prefetching on two GPU
platforms [8]. That operator differs from our two-direction
3 × 3 L1-magnitude workload. Stencil auto-tuning [9] and
Halide’s separation of image algorithms from schedules [10]
establish that locality transformations and execution sched-
ules are longstanding optimization topics. Shared memory is
therefore an experimentally evaluated candidate here, not an
asserted contribution.
Qilin uses adaptive CPU–GPU mapping [11]. StarPU
supplies heterogeneous task scheduling and data manage-
ment [12]; SKMD partitions OpenCL kernels while accounting
for transfers and device performance [13]. Cooperative CUDA
execution [14], Fluidic Kernels [15], and performance-aware
image-analysis scheduling [16] further establish this ﬁeld.
A survey organizes cooperative CPU–GPU techniques [17].
Particularly close recent work by Huang et al. models stage-
level CPU/GPU Canny conﬁgurations with explicit transfer
costs and latency objectives [18]. Our small row-split model
is substantially narrower and is not a replacement for these
systems.
